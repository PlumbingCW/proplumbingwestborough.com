How to Replace a Toilet Float and Fill Valve (Westborough, MA Homeowner Guide)

A toilet that hisses, runs on, or refills every few minutes usually has a worn fill valve or a float set at the wrong height. The fix takes about thirty minutes and one inexpensive part. The steps below cover a standard two-piece toilet with a tank on the back.

If your toilet has a pressure-assist tank or a wall-hung carrier, skip this guide and call a plumber. Those designs hide their valves inside sealed components.

Confirm the fill valve is the problem

Lift the tank lid and set it somewhere safe, because porcelain lids chip. Flush and watch the refill. The water should rise to a mark about an inch below the top of the overflow tube, then stop. If water keeps trickling into the overflow tube, or you hear a hiss long after the tank looks full, the fill valve is not closing.

Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank and do not flush for fifteen minutes. If color shows in the bowl, the leak is the flapper at the bottom, not the fill valve. Many running toilets have both problems, so check the flapper before you buy parts.

Pick the right float style

Fill valves come in a few designs. The float is the part that rises with the water and tells the valve to shut.

Common fill valve and float designs
DesignHow the float worksGood to know
Cup float, adjustableA cup slides up the valve body on the water surfaceMost common today, easy to adjust by hand.
Ball float on armA hollow ball on a rod lifts a plunger valveOlder style, rods can bend, parts are harder to find.
Diaphragm valve with side floatA rubber diaphragm seals the outletQuiet, common on mid-range toilets.
Internal pilot valveWater level moves a small pilot inside the bodyFewer exposed parts, but cannot be adjusted as easily.

Gather tools and shut things down

Collect everything before the water is off so the toilet is not out of service longer than necessary.

  • A new universal fill valve rated for your tank height.
  • A new braided supply line if the old one is stiff or discolored.
  • An adjustable wrench and a pair of pliers.
  • A small bucket, a sponge, and two towels for the floor.
  • A flashlight, because the shutoff valve sits in a shadow.
  • A pencil to mark the water line on the tank before you drain it.

Remove the old valve

Close the angle stop behind the toilet by turning it clockwise until it stops. Flush once to empty the tank, then sponge out the last inch of water. Loosen the nut that holds the supply line to the bottom of the old fill valve and set the line aside.

Under the tank, a large plastic lock nut holds the valve in place. Unscrew it by hand or with pliers, then lift the valve out through the top. Keep a towel under the connection because a little water will drip out of the tank bolts and the valve base.

Install the new fill valve and set the level

Adjust the new valve to the tank height first. Most universal valves twist or telescope to a height marked on the body. The top of the valve should sit at least an inch above the top of the overflow tube so the water can never siphon back into the supply.

Drop the valve through the hole in the tank, tighten the lock nut by hand plus a quarter turn, and attach the supply line. Clip the small refill tube from the valve onto the overflow tube. Point it into the tube, not down the side of the tank, or it may keep the bowl filling after the tank is full.

Open the angle stop slowly. When the tank fills, turn the adjustment screw or slide the cup float until the waterline lands about an inch below the top of the overflow tube. Flush three times and watch the valve shut each time. If it hisses at shutoff, the water pressure may be pushing the diaphragm, and a different valve model may be needed.

When to stop and call a plumber

Call for help if the angle stop leaks when you turn it, if the supply nut will not loosen, or if the tank bolts are corroded and spin freely. Those jobs can turn a thirty-minute repair into a flooded bathroom.

Why it matters at the tank: mineral buildup around the fill valve seat and a dirty flapper seal both cause silent leaks. A running toilet can waste many gallons a day without any visible puddle, so the monthly water bill is often the first sign. Compare your bill with last year's, and if it climbed with no change in your household, read the meter with every fixture off.

Toilet float and fill valve replacement: questions answered

Most fill valves last seven to ten years. Hard or gritty water and chloramine can shorten that. Replace the valve and the supply line together, since both wear on the same schedule.

A phantom flush usually means the flapper is letting water seep into the bowl. The tank drops a little, the fill valve opens, and it refills. Replace the flapper first.

A like-for-like part swap inside the tank is routine maintenance. For anything that moves or adds piping, permit rules vary, so ask the Westborough Building Department.
