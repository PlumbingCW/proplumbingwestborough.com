Water is rising in the pit: what to do first

Stay out of a flooded basement if water has reached outlets, appliances or the electrical panel. Standing water and electricity together can kill, so leave the cutoff to the utility or an electrician when in doubt.

If the floor is dry and safe, lift the lid and check the pump. A float stuck against the wall of the pit is the most common cause and sometimes a nudge restarts the pump. If it does not start, unplug it and call us.

  • Do not wade into water that touches the panel, outlets or appliances.
  • Check that the pump is plugged in and the outlet breaker is not tripped.
  • Lift the float by hand, since a free float should switch the pump on.
  • Bail or wet-vac the pit if the water is low and the area is safe.
  • Move stored items off the floor and photograph the water line for your insurer.

Sump pump versus ejector pump

A sump pump removes groundwater that gathers in a pit at the lowest point of the basement, and it discharges outside the house. An ejector pump moves wastewater from a basement bathroom or laundry up to the sewer line, in a sealed basin.

The two look alike and fail differently. A sump pump failure floods with clean groundwater. An ejector failure backs up sewage, which is a health hazard and needs prompt professional cleanup.

If your basement has a bathroom, find out which pump you have and where the alarm is. Our sewer line repair service handles the line an ejector discharges into.

Groundwater near Westborough's ponds and headwaters

Westborough holds the headwaters of the Sudbury and Assabet rivers, along with Lake Chauncy, Sandra Pond and Hocomonco Pond. A town with this much surface water can have groundwater that rises seasonally in some neighborhoods.

That is a general point and not a prediction for your address. If your basement has ever been damp after snowmelt or a heavy rain, assume a sump pump matters, and ask us whether the pit, the pump and the discharge are sized for it.

Choosing and sizing a new pump

Pumps are rated by horsepower and by how many gallons per hour they move at a given lift. A one-third horsepower unit suits many homes, and a larger half-horsepower pump suits deep pits, long discharge runs or heavy inflow.

A cast iron pump body dissipates heat better than plastic, and a vertical float switch fits a narrow pit. A check valve on the discharge keeps water from falling back into the pit after each cycle.

We size the pump to the pit and the lift, and we run it with water before we leave.

A backup plan for the storm that kills the power

The storms that flood basements are often the ones that cut the power. A backup removes that single point of failure.

  • Install a battery backup pump that runs when the main pump or the grid fails.
  • Add a high-water alarm that sounds before the pit overflows.
  • Test the pump twice a year by pouring in a bucket of water.
  • Keep the discharge pipe clear of snow, ice and leaves in winter.
  • Know where your main water valve is, since a burst pipe can swamp a pit too.

Discharge, drains and the storm drain question

Where the pump discharges matters. Water should leave the house well away from the foundation and onto ground that slopes away, never into the sanitary sewer. Rules about connecting to a storm drain vary, so ask the Westborough Building Department.

Storm drain cleaning is a separate service for yards and catch basins. If water pools by the foundation after rain even with a working pump, our basement waterproofing page explains the next steps.
