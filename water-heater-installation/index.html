Start with the room, not the model

Where the heater lives sets the options. A basement with a floor drain and an open flue is the easy case. A second-floor closet, an attic or a finished laundry room limits fuel choices and adds drain pan and discharge requirements.

We look at the existing location first, then the gas line or electrical panel, then the venting path. Only after that do we talk about brands and capacity, because the best unit is the one that fits the house you have.

Choosing between four kinds of heater

Four categories cover almost every residential install. The table summarizes how they differ for a typical Westborough home.

TypeBest fitWatch for
Gas storage tankHomes already on natural gas with a flueVenting condition and combustion air.

The pre-install checklist we run through

Everything on this list is verified before the old unit is touched. It prevents the surprises that lengthen an install day.

  • Fuel type and supply: gas line size and shutoff, or electrical breaker and wire gauge.
  • Flue or vent route, including clearances to combustibles and any change of pipe material.
  • Water pressure and whether the system already has an expansion tank.
  • Drain or pan: where the relief discharge pipe will run and where a leak would go.
  • Floor and strapping: the unit must sit on a level, stable base.
  • Access for hauling the old tank out and the new tank in.

Venting and combustion air on gas units

A gas water heater needs fresh air to burn and a clear path to send exhaust out. Tight, well-insulated modern houses can starve a draft-hood unit of air, which pulls exhaust back into the room.

When the air or vent path looks marginal, a sealed combustion or power-vent model is the safer choice. It costs more, and it removes a risk you cannot see. We test for carbon monoxide after the install and confirm your detectors work.

Water quality and what goes into the new tank

Most of Westborough is on the town water system, which MassDEP's assessment lists as nine wells and one reservoir with fluoride and corrosion control treatment. We have no hardness figure to quote, so test your tap or read the current annual water quality report if you want to plan for scale.

Hopkinton is a different case. About forty percent of its residents rely on private wells according to local reporting, and well water can carry iron and manganese that foul a heating element or a tankless heat exchanger. If you are on a private well, have the water tested by a certified lab before choosing a heater. Our Hopkinton plumbing page covers that side of the line.

Permits, codes and who signs off

In Massachusetts, plumbing and gas fitting are regulated trades. The Uniform State Plumbing Code is 248 CMR 10.00, and gas work follows 248 CMR 4.00 and its NFPA 54 amendments in 248 CMR 5.00. Permit rules vary, so ask the Westborough Building Department what your project requires.

We tell you before work begins who pulls the permit and what the inspector will look at. A homeowner can verify any plumber through the state Check a license tool on mass.gov.

Planning a swap to tankless or a heat pump

Moving off a conventional tank changes more than the appliance. A tankless gas unit usually needs a larger gas line and new venting, and a heat pump tank needs space and a condensate route. Each can be the right move, and each is a bigger project.

Our tankless water heater page explains flushing, descaling and installation in detail. If your existing tank is leaking now, read about water heater repair to see whether it can limp through until your install date.

What happens on installation day

A planned install usually runs half a day for a like-for-like swap and longer for a fuel change.

  • Arrival call, floor protection down and the old tank drained.
  • Old unit disconnected, removed and hauled away.
  • New heater set, leveled and piped with new connectors and shutoffs.
  • Gas, venting and relief discharge tested, with carbon monoxide readings recorded.
  • Thermostat set and instructions explained, including where the shutoffs are.
  • Walkthrough and cleanup before the plumber leaves.

What sets the price of an installation

We quote after seeing the room, and the number you approve is the number you pay.

Unit type and capacityTankless and hybrid units cost more up front than a standard tank of the same household rating.
Venting changesPower-vent or sealed-combustion systems add materials and labor compared with a reused flue.
Gas or electrical upgradesA bigger gas line or a new circuit is priced separately from the heater.
Permit and inspectionFees and required paperwork vary by town, so we do not quote a figure until the scope is clear.
Removal and disposalOld tanks are heavy, and difficult stairs or tight turns add time.

Insurance does not pay for a planned upgrade. If a fixed unit fails and causes sudden water damage, the cleanup may be covered, but the replacement tank usually is not.
