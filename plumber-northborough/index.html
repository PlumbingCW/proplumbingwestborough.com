The water bill jumped and nothing is dripping

In 2021 the town delivered 326 million gallons to 4,233 accounts, according to the MWRA's Northborough community page. Divide one number by the other and the average account used about 77,000 gallons that year, or roughly 210 gallons a day. That average includes businesses, so a quiet household should sit below it, but it gives you a rough yardstick.

If your own use climbs well past your normal pattern, suspect a hidden leak before you suspect the meter. A toilet flapper that seeps into the bowl can waste hundreds of gallons a day without a sound. A cracked line behind a wall or under a slab can waste far more.

A leak detection visit starts at the meter. We confirm the dial moves with every fixture off, then use acoustic listening gear and a thermal camera to mark the spot before anyone cuts into drywall.

A fifteen minute check before you call about a bill

These steps take no tools and tell dispatch a great deal when you call.

  • Turn off every tap and appliance, read the meter, then read it again 15 minutes later. A moving dial means water is leaving the house.
  • Add a few drops of food coloring to each toilet tank and wait without flushing. Color in the bowl points to a worn flapper.
  • Look at the base of the water heater and the discharge pipe from its relief valve for moisture.
  • Check washing machine hoses and the shutoff valves under each sink for crust, drips or bulging rubber.
  • Listen near the main shutoff for a faint hiss while everything is off.
  • If the meter keeps moving after all of that, close the main valve and call the 24/7 line.

Drains that back up on the lowest floor first

Water follows gravity, so a blocked main line shows itself at the lowest drain in the house. A basement floor drain that gurgles when the washing machine empties, or a shower that rises when someone flushes upstairs, usually points past the individual fixture.

One slow sink is normally a trap or branch problem and responds to drain cleaning with a cable. When two or more fixtures complain at once, the stoppage is probably in the 4-inch main, and a camera run along with sewer line cleaning tells us whether roots, grease or a collapsed section is the cause.

Stop running water while you wait. Every gallon you send down a blocked main has to go somewhere, and it often ends up on the basement floor.

No hot water on a weekday morning

A tank heater that has stopped heating usually has one of four faults: a tripped breaker or dead igniter, a failed thermostat, a burned element, or a tank that has rusted through. The first three are repairs. A tank that leaks from the shell is finished.

Most tank heaters serve for roughly 8 to 12 years, so a unit at that age with a wet floor beneath it is a replacement call. We explain both routes on the water heater repair page, and the replacement page covers sizing, venting and expansion tanks.

Turn the gas valve or the breaker off before you mop. Standing water around a powered heater is a shock hazard.

Before we arrive in Northborough

Four moves limit the damage while a plumber is on the way.

Close the main valve

Find it where the line enters near the meter. Read the meter once more after closing it. If the dial still moves, the leak is ahead of your valve and the town should know.

Cut power near the water

Turn off breakers for any room with standing water or a wet ceiling. Do not touch outlets with wet hands.

Clear the area

Move rugs, boxes and electronics out of the wet zone and open cabinet doors under the affected sink.

Call the 24/7 line

Describe what you see, which floor it is on and whether the water is stopped. Photos for insurance can wait until the flow is controlled.
