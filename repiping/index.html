When repairs stop making sense

Most homeowners resist repiping until the third or fourth leak. A repair is the right answer for one failure in otherwise sound pipe. A pattern changes the math, because each patch costs a visit and the next leak is usually close by.

  • Pinhole leaks appear again in different spots along the same pipe.
  • Water runs brown or rusty first thing in the morning.
  • Pressure has dropped at every fixture and the main valve is fully open.
  • Visible corrosion, green crusts or flaking scale shows at joints and fittings.
  • The house is old and you cannot confirm what the pipes are made of.

What your old pipes may be made of

Homes built before the 1960s may have galvanized steel supply lines, which rust from the inside and slowly choke off flow. Houses from the mid-century onward more often have copper, and newer work may use PEX or CPVC. Some very old homes may still carry lead in a service line or old solder joints, since lead solder was banned in 1986.

We cannot tell the material from the outside of a wall, so we look at pipes in the basement or crawl space, near the water heater, and at the main entry. We also check pressure and flow. These readings show if the problem is localized or system-wide.

PEX versus copper: choosing a new pipe

Two materials dominate repipe jobs, and either can be a good choice. Cost, installation time and how the pipe handles freezing differ. The table summarizes what matters most to homeowners.

Whole house or one section

A whole-house repipe replaces every supply line from the main to each fixture. A partial repipe replaces one branch, a bathroom group, or the lines in a single wall. Partial work makes sense when the rest of the system is in good shape and the failure is limited to one area.

We tell you the tradeoff honestly. A partial job costs less today, while a whole-house job removes the chance of another section failing in two years. If you are selling the house or planning a remodel, doing the repipe at the same time usually saves you from opening the same walls twice.

How a repipe runs from start to finish

The job begins with a walk-through, a pressure reading and a plan for where each new line will run. We place access cuts in closets, behind fixtures and in unfinished areas so wall repair is limited. The water is off while the work is done, so we schedule around your household and tell you how long you will be without it.

New lines are pressure tested before walls are closed. Local permit rules vary, so ask the Westborough Building Department what is required, and your plumber will handle the paperwork for work on your property. Licensed plumbers work under the Massachusetts Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters, and you can verify any license with the state Check a license tool.

Older houses and historic buildings

Westborough incorporated in 1717, and parts of town include the Vintonville and West Main Street historic districts. Owners of older buildings face extra care, because plaster, original trim and unusual framing make cutting harder. A good repipe plan keeps cuts small and uses existing chases when possible.

The practical step is to tell the plumber about the age and any preservation concerns on the first call. Photos of the basement or crawl space help us estimate how many walls need access, and what finishing work you will need to arrange afterward.

Mill villages, mixed materials and what Grafton homeowners see

The Blackstone River villages of Grafton, including Farnumsville, Fisherville and Saundersville, grew around mills and may contain older housing with several generations of plumbing. Mixed pipe materials, galvanized steel and patched copper are common in homes of that age. Our Grafton emergency plumber page explains how we cover the villages.

If your house is in a town like that, the same advice applies: have the pipes identified before you commit to a plan. The goal is not to replace for the sake of replacing, but to replace what is failing.

Water chemistry and the life of new pipe

The Westborough Water Department uses corrosion control, according to a MassDEP source water assessment that lists potassium hydroxide and zinc orthophosphate. That assessment is older, so check the current annual water quality report. Water chemistry matters because it affects how fast metal pipe wears from the inside.

If you plan a repipe after repeated pinhole leaks, show the report to your plumber. It helps us decide between copper and PEX, and whether to look at related equipment like a pressure regulator. For the immediate problem of a split pipe, see burst pipe repair, and for slow hidden losses see leak detection.
