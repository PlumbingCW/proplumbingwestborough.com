Start with the source: four ways water enters

Surface water arrives from roof runoff and grading that slopes toward the house. Hydrostatic pressure pushes groundwater through the floor and the joint between the floor and the wall, called the cove joint.

Wall cracks and tie holes admit water during heavy rain, and a plumbing leak can imitate all of these. Our leak detection work rules a pipe out before we recommend any waterproofing.

Condensation on cold walls in summer is a fifth source, and it is a ventilation and dehumidifier problem, not a leak.

Free fixes before any system

Many damp basements improve with simple outdoor work. Try these steps before you spend on a system.

  • Extend downspouts at least several feet from the foundation.
  • Regrade soil so it falls away from the wall.
  • Clear gutters and check for overflowing corners.
  • Seal visible wall cracks only as a stopgap, not a cure.
  • Run a dehumidifier and track the humidity to separate condensation from leaks.

How an interior drain and pit work together

An interior system collects water at the footing and routes it to a sump pit, from which a pump discharges it outside. The drain sits in a trench cut along the perimeter, below the slab, and the floor is patched afterward.

The pit and pump are what make it work, so the pump is the system. If the pump fails, the water rises. That is why we pair waterproofing with a sound sump pump installation and a battery backup.

Westborough's water setting, kept honest

Westborough contains the headwaters of the Sudbury and Assabet rivers, plus Lake Chauncy, Sandra Pond and Hocomonco Pond. A landscape with that much open water can produce wet ground in spring.

The town's geography alone cannot tell us if your basement will leak. The honest approach is to watch the basement through a wet season, note where and when water appears, and let that guide the repair.

Membranes, coatings and what paint cannot do

Waterproof paint and masonry sealers can slow dampness from vapor, but they cannot hold back water under pressure and they peel when it pushes through. A membrane system behind the wall surface gives water a path to the drain instead.

Wall membranes also protect finishes if you plan to finish the basement. We install them where the wall is moist and the floor drain system is in place.

If mold is present, remediation is a separate trade, and we leave that to a qualified mold contractor.

Check your basement during a storm

The most useful information you can give any contractor is where the water appeared and when. Walk the basement during the next heavy rain with a flashlight and note the wall, the floor joint or the window well.

Take a photo, mark the spot with painter's tape and record the weather. That evidence lets us separate a gutter problem from a groundwater problem, and it may save you money.
